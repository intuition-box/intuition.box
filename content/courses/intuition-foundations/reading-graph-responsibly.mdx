Reading Intuition data is not a neutral act. The moment an application filters claims, resolves metadata, ranks results, or chooses a label, it turns protocol data into a product interpretation.

That is necessary—raw data is rarely a good interface. The responsibility is to make the transformation understandable and avoid claiming more authority than the evidence supports.

## Three levels of a read

It helps to separate:

1. **Protocol state:** accepted state transitions, identifiers, positions, and events governed by the protocol.
2. **Indexed representation:** a query-friendly model derived from protocol activity, potentially with latency or calculated fields.
3. **Application view:** selection, aggregation, ranking, labels, and explanations chosen for a specific audience.

If a product shows “trusted by the community,” that phrase is not raw protocol state. It is a conclusion based on a definition of community, a weighting method, a time window, and perhaps excluded data. Those assumptions should be inspectable.

## A responsible claim view

Depending on context, a useful presentation may include:

- the subject, predicate, and object;
- who created or supported the claim;
- supporting and opposing positions;
- when relevant activity occurred;
- linked evidence or related claims;
- concentration or diversity of signal;
- the application's ranking or verification policy.

Not every screen needs every field. Progressive disclosure can keep an interface calm while giving users a path to examine provenance.

## Recency and change

Knowledge ages. “Maintains project” can stop being true. “Audited version” may not cover the current release. A signal made before new evidence appeared has a different meaning afterward. Applications should use timestamps and scope instead of presenting all historical claims as equally current.

## The ethics of labels

Small words carry large assumptions. Compare:

- **verified** — implies a defined verification procedure;
- **supported** — indicates positive positions without proving accuracy;
- **popular** — describes attention but not quality;
- **recommended by maintainers** — specifies a source group;
- **high signal** — describes protocol state while leaving interpretation open.

Precise labels make the product more trustworthy because they preserve the boundary between observation and inference.

<CaseStudy title="Two honest interfaces, two different rankings">
  A package explorer ranks by recent maintainer support. A security dashboard ranks the same claims by signals from recognized auditors and penalizes stale evidence. Both use the shared graph; neither ranking is protocol truth. Each can be responsible if its policy and inputs are clear.
</CaseStudy>
